---
title: "Interest Is Not Demand"
description: "Builders Commune taught me the difference between people liking an idea and people being willing to change their behaviour for it."
date: 2026-06-09
category: Product
---

There was a point when I thought Builders Commune was a very good idea. To be fair, I still think it was a good idea. The problem was that I confused a good idea with something people wanted badly enough to actually show up for.

Builders Commune was supposed to bring builders together. The idea was simple: create a space where people working on businesses, software, community projects and other interesting ideas could meet, exchange ideas, collaborate and hopefully build useful things together. Whenever I described it to people, the reaction was almost always positive. People said we needed something like that. They said they would come. They said they loved the concept, and at the time that felt like validation.

Then I organised it, and the room did not fill up with all the enthusiastic people who had told me how necessary the idea was. Most of the people who actually showed up were my friends, plus a boyfriend who came under some level of emotional duress because, well, relationships come with terms and conditions.

It was funny, but it was also useful. Builders Commune gave me one of the clearest lessons I have learned about building anything: **interest is not demand**.

Someone saying they like your idea costs them nothing. Someone telling you they would use your product costs them nothing. Someone saying they would attend your event costs them nothing. Even when they are being completely sincere, they are still describing an intention, not demonstrating behaviour.

Demand starts to become interesting when something is required from the person making the choice. It might be money, but it can also be time, effort, inconvenience, attention or the discomfort of changing an existing habit. That is when you begin to see whether the thing matters enough.

A person who says they would attend an event is giving you one kind of signal. A person who registers, makes the trip across town and actually arrives is giving you a much stronger one. A person who says they would pay for a product is giving you useful feedback, but a person who enters their card details is telling you something much more concrete. The closer you get to real behaviour, the better the information becomes.

I think founders confuse compliments with evidence all the time because compliments feel good and evidence is much less polite. We often describe an idea to people who already know us, explain the problem carefully, explain why our solution makes sense and then ask whether they would use it. By that point, we have practically written the answer for them. Of course many people say yes.

The problem is that people are not particularly good at predicting what they will do later, especially when the hypothetical decision has no cost attached to it. I can sincerely believe that I would attend something every Saturday, subscribe to a useful service, open an app every morning or change the way I buy something. Then Saturday comes and I am tired. The subscription page asks for my card. The app requires me to create an account. The alternative I already use is easier. Real life introduces friction that did not exist when I was answering the question, and that gap between what people say and what they actually do is where a lot of the truth lives.

> Compliments feel good and evidence is much less polite.
{: .pull-quote aria-hidden="true"}

Builders Commune made me much more interested in testing behaviour early. If I want to know whether people will attend something, I would rather open registration than collect enthusiastic comments. If I want to know whether someone would pay, a deposit tells me more than a survey answer. If an organisation says a programme sounds useful, I pay more attention to whether they introduce the decision maker, commit resources or agree to a pilot than to how positive the meeting felt.

None of this means that early interest is useless. Interest matters. It tells you that the idea resonates enough for someone to stop and listen. It can help you understand how people describe the problem, what language they respond to and which part of the idea attracts them. It just does not tell you the same thing as demand.

That distinction matters even more when you are someone who enjoys building, because building creates its own temptation. If you can code, design or organise, it is easy to move straight from an interesting idea to making the thing. Building feels productive because something visible exists at the end of it. Research is less satisfying, especially when it tells you that the thing you were excited to create may not matter very much to anyone else. But ten people saying an idea is brilliant do not become customers just because you turn the idea into software.

A polished website does not create demand. A beautiful brand does not create demand. A technically impressive product does not create demand. Your friends showing up because they care about you definitely does not create demand either, although I remain very grateful to mine.

What I care about much more now is the moment when someone has to make a real choice. A stranger signs up without needing to be convinced personally. A customer pays. A school commits. A partner allocates resources. A user returns without being reminded. Someone chooses your product even though doing nothing would have been easier. Those are the moments I now count as evidence.

Builders Commune was not some dramatic failure. It was simply one of those experiences that changes the way you interpret enthusiasm afterwards. I still appreciate it when someone tells me they love an idea. I just no longer confuse that with proof that the idea has a market.

These days, when someone says, “I would absolutely use this,” part of me is already wondering what happens when there is a button in front of them. Interest sounds good, but demand behaves differently, and the button is usually where you find out which one you have.
