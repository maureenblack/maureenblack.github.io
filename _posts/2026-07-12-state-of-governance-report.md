Everyone in Cardano governance has an opinion about Cardano governance. That is not a criticism, because governance is supposed to produce opinions. The problem is that an ecosystem cannot make every governance decision based on whichever opinion is loudest in the room.

That was one of the reasons the Beyond Minimum Viable Governance work mattered to me. Instead of beginning with a theory about what Cardano's governance problem was, the project brought together interviews, workshops, surveys and on chain data to understand what people were actually experiencing. The resulting **Cardano State of Governance Report** is a community informed snapshot of where the system stands, what participants are struggling with and which problems deserve more attention. You can [read the full report here](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1INAVHFd8MSvitdQ6oKzxqdyLqx-jhGGu/view){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

One of the findings that stood out was DRep voting power concentration. On chain analysis showed that voting power was becoming more concentrated over the period studied. That finding eventually became part of the evidence behind [CPS-0033](https://github.com/cardano-foundation/CIPs/blob/master/CPS-0033/README.md){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, the Cardano Problem Statement on DRep Voting Power Concentration that I co-authored with Danielle Stanko.

But concentration was not the only thing participants were talking about. The report also highlighted fragmented tooling, information overload and accessibility barriers that make governance participation harder than it needs to be. Participants raised questions about incentives, wallet UX, DRep discovery, governance tooling and the long term sustainability of governance roles.

Those findings matter because Cardano now has a functioning governance system. The question is no longer simply whether decentralised governance can be implemented at protocol level, because it can. Now we have to ask whether normal participants can meaningfully use it, and that is a harder problem.

A governance system can be decentralised in architecture while becoming concentrated in practice. It can be transparent while remaining difficult to understand. It can allow participation while making participation so time consuming that only a narrow group can sustain it.

I think this is why research like the State of Governance work matters: it gives us a baseline. Without that, every debate risks becoming anecdotal. Someone says participation is healthy because many DReps are registered, and someone else says participation is weak because only a small number are consistently active. Someone says concentration is normal because people choose trusted representatives, while someone else looks at the same concentration and sees a threat to decentralisation.

Research does not magically resolve those disagreements. It makes the disagreement more precise, and that is a much better place to start.
