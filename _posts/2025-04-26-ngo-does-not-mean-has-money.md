One of the stranger things about building Giiyo Tech has been discovering how quickly people make assumptions about money the moment they hear the word “organisation”.

Sometimes it happens with schools. We approach a school because we want to run a programme for their students at no cost to the school, and somehow the conversation still turns into what we are going to pay them. Other times somebody hears that Giiyo works with young people across different cities and assumes there must be a large donor somewhere financing everything. For a long time, there was not. A significant amount of Giiyo was built because I kept paying for things myself.

Transport still costs money when your programme is free. Equipment costs money. Printing costs money. Internet costs money. Volunteers still need to get from one place to another. A robotics workshop does not become free because the children attending it are not paying.

This creates a very strange situation for small social impact organisations. The people receiving the work see an organisation. The organisation itself might still be one person moving money from another part of her life to make sure Saturday's programme happens.

I understand where some of the assumptions come from. Cameroon has had NGOs and international development organisations operating here for decades. People have seen projects arrive with branded vehicles, per diems, large workshops and donor budgets. Over time, “NGO” can start to sound less like a legal or organisational structure and more like a synonym for “somebody has brought money”.

That creates bad incentives on both sides. Schools and communities may start asking what they are receiving financially before asking whether the programme is useful. Small organisations then feel pressure to imitate the behaviour of large funded institutions in order to be taken seriously. Founders begin spending money on appearances instead of the actual work because legitimacy is often judged by what an organisation seems able to spend. Giiyo has forced me to think much more carefully about that.

Our experience has also changed depending on where we work. In Douala, school partnerships have sometimes required far more convincing than I expected. In Limbe, I found schools considerably more open to discussing what we wanted to do and how their students could benefit. In Yaoundé, having people inside a school who genuinely want a programme makes an enormous difference. The organisation, the mission and the programmes are the same in all three places, but the response can be completely different.

That is why I have become wary of explanations that reduce everything to “schools do not understand NGOs” or “people just want money”. The reality is more complicated. Trust, previous experiences, local relationships and language all matter, and so does the person sitting on the other side of the table.

But the financial assumption is real, and small organisations need to be honest about it. A nonprofit does not mean nobody gets paid, and it does not mean money is irrelevant. It means the organisation does not exist to distribute profit to owners. Good programmes still need budgets. Staff should eventually be paid properly. Equipment has to be purchased, systems cost money and growth costs money. The difference is where the money goes and what the organisation exists to do.

This matters to me because I do not want Giiyo to build a culture where being mission driven means pretending resources are unnecessary. That usually ends with founders burning themselves out, volunteers carrying responsibilities indefinitely without support, and programmes depending on personal sacrifice instead of sustainable systems. I also do not want us to become the kind of organisation that throws money at every institution simply to secure access.

If a programme genuinely benefits a school and its students, there should be room for a partnership in which both sides contribute something. Sometimes our contribution is equipment, curriculum and facilitators. The school's contribution might be space, time, teachers or coordination. Not every relationship has to begin with cash changing hands.

Giiyo is getting older now. We are talking to larger partners, building more formal programmes and thinking about infrastructure such as the Giiyo STEM Centre in Limbe. That means the organisation will increasingly need real funding, and I want that. Funding would let us do more, do it better and stop relying so heavily on personal sacrifice.

But I hope we never confuse having a bigger budget with becoming more legitimate. Giiyo was legitimate when the work was being paid for from my pocket too. The children still learned, the robots still moved and the code still ran. The organisation simply had less money, and having less money is not the same thing as being less legitimate.
