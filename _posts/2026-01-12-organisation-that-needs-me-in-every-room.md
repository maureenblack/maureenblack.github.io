For a long time, being needed everywhere felt like evidence that I was doing a good job. If there was a school meeting, I should be there. If a programme was being designed, I should review it. If something was being posted publicly, I wanted to see it. If a volunteer had a question, it eventually came to me.

When you start an organisation, that is almost unavoidable. The organisation begins as information inside your head. You know why decisions were made, who promised what, how much money is available, which relationships are sensitive and what the organisation is trying to become.

The problem is that a founder can become very good at compensating for the absence of systems. I remember what nobody wrote down. I follow up on something because I happen to know it is late. I notice that two people are working from different assumptions and connect them. From the outside, the organisation appears to function, but internally the operating system is still one person's memory, and that does not scale.

Giiyo Tech has made this very obvious to me as we have started working across more programmes, cities and partnerships. If something is happening in Limbe, something else is being organised in Yaoundé and another conversation is happening somewhere else, I cannot be the connection between all of them forever. More importantly, I do not want to be. I want Giiyo to become an organisation where good work happens when I am not in the room.

That requires a different kind of leadership from the version where the founder solves everything. It means being clear about who actually owns something: not who is helping or who is in the WhatsApp group, but who is responsible for moving it forward. It also means information has to leave my head. People need access to documents, expectations, programme plans, partner context and decisions without having to ask me for the history every time. This sounds obvious when written down, but it is surprisingly difficult to practise.

Founders receive a lot of emotional reward from being indispensable. People come to you because you know everything. Decisions wait for you. Your presence appears to make things move. Then one day you realise you have accidentally built yourself a job that nobody else can perform and an organisation that becomes weaker every time you get busy. That is not control. It is fragility.

Giiyo is now at the stage where I care more about designing the system than proving that I can personally carry the system. That means some things may initially be done differently from how I would do them. People need room to make decisions, learn and occasionally make small mistakes without the founder appearing immediately to take over.

It also means accountability has to become clearer. Delegation is not dumping a task on someone and hoping for the best. If somebody owns something, they need enough context, authority and resources to actually own it, otherwise the task eventually returns to the founder anyway.

I think this matters especially for organisations built around a mission, where there is always more work than capacity. Another school could be contacted, another learner could be supported, another partnership could be followed up and another programme could be designed. If the founder becomes the answer to every gap, there is no natural limit until the founder becomes exhausted. The organisation needs systems strong enough to say, “This is how we work,” even when the person who started it is somewhere else.

That is what I want for Giiyo. It is not because I care less about the organisation. It is because I care enough about it to want it to survive my absence.
