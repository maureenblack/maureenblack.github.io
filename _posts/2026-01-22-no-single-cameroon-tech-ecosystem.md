People often talk about “the Cameroon tech ecosystem” as though it is one coherent place. I understand why. Countries are convenient units for reports, conferences and funding programmes, and it is easier to say “Cameroon” than to explain the very different environments inside Cameroon. But the more I build here, the less useful that phrase becomes without context.

Douala does not behave like Limbe, Limbe does not behave like Yaoundé, and Bamenda brings a completely different set of realities again. The differences are not merely geographical. Language, institutional culture and networks change from place to place, and so do what people consider trustworthy and the kinds of opportunities young people have already been exposed to. Even the process of getting a school to answer your message can be completely different.

Giiyo taught me this in a very practical way. We had periods in Douala where trying to form school partnerships felt unnecessarily difficult. We were offering programmes intended to benefit students, sometimes at no cost to the school, yet conversations could still be slow, sceptical or focused on what financial benefit the institution itself would receive. Then I went to Limbe and found a noticeably different level of openness.

That does not mean schools in Limbe are inherently better or that people in Douala do not care about STEM. It means local context matters more than broad labels. In Limbe, English also makes communication easier for me. Relationships form differently, schools may have different experiences with outside organisations, and leadership styles vary.

Yaoundé introduces another dynamic again. When you have an internal teacher or school contact who already understands why the programme matters, the entire relationship changes. You are no longer selling the idea from zero, because somebody inside the institution is helping translate the opportunity into its own context.

This is why I get uncomfortable when international programmes enter a country through one city, work with one type of institution and then describe what “Cameroonian youth” need. It matters which young people they mean and where they live, what language they learn in, what access they have to computers and the internet, how much support their school gives them and what their parents expect of them.

Even the technology community itself is fragmented. Someone participating in blockchain governance from Cameroon may have very little overlap with a teenager learning HTML in Bamenda or a university student trying to enter cybersecurity. They are all part of “tech in Cameroon”, but that does not make their environments interchangeable.

I actually think this diversity is useful if we pay attention to it, because working across places forces you to question assumptions. A programme model that works in Douala may need different partnerships in Limbe. Something that works beautifully in an English speaking environment may need more than translation to work in French. A digital platform that feels accessible to urban professionals may be unusable in the schools you claim it was designed to serve.

This is one reason Giiyo's long term work cannot simply be about scaling the same workshop everywhere. We need local partners and people on the ground, and we need to learn which parts of a programme should remain consistent and which parts need to change. The country is one country, but the ecosystem is not one room.
