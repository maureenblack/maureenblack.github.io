Knowing how to build software is an incredible advantage when you have ideas. You do not need to convince an engineer to join you. You do not need to raise money just to test whether something can exist. You can open your laptop tonight and have the beginning of the product tomorrow. I love that, but it is also dangerous.

When you cannot build something yourself, friction forces you to justify the idea. You may need to find a technical cofounder, pay a developer or persuade somebody that the problem matters enough to work on. That friction can kill good ideas, but it also kills a lot of bad ones, and developers do not have that protection.

If I become excited about an idea at 11 p.m., nobody has to approve the budget, ask whether users want it or tell me the market is too small. I can simply start. By 3 a.m. there is a repository, two days later there is authentication, and a week later I am emotionally attached to a database schema for a product nobody asked for.

That is the dangerous part. Once you have built enough of something, it becomes very easy to confuse the amount of work already invested with evidence that you should continue. You have code now. You have a logo, and you bought the domain. You have spent so much time solving technical problems that abandoning the idea begins to feel like wasting the work, so you keep building.

I have become much more suspicious of that instinct. Being able to build quickly is only an advantage if you are also willing to stop quickly. The real superpower is not that I can take an idea from my head to working software without waiting for someone else. The real superpower would be being able to do that without falling in love with the fact that I built it, and that is harder.

[Koki](https://kokiafrique.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} taught me a healthier version of this. The app eventually launched, which meant the work stopped being purely about what I could technically make and started becoming about how real users interacted with it. [Builders Commune](/blog/interest-is-not-demand/) taught me the other side: a concept can sound good and attract interest without creating enough behavioural demand to justify continuing exactly as imagined.

Those experiences made me care much more about the point before the code. Before I build now, I want to know who needs the thing, what they are doing at the moment, what evidence I have that the current situation bothers them enough to change, and whether I can test the idea without spending three months engineering it.

Sometimes the answer is still to build it. That is the beautiful thing about being technical: I can learn things by making prototypes other people would need a budget to create. But sometimes the better experiment is a form, a conversation, a manual service, a deposit, a spreadsheet or an ugly page with one button.

The goal of validation is not to prove that I am capable of building the idea. I already know I can build things. The goal is to learn whether the thing deserves to be built.

There is also another danger in being technical: you can solve problems users never cared about. Engineers notice inefficiencies. We see architecture that could be cleaner, workflows that could be automated and systems that could be more elegant, and then we assume improving those things creates value. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the user was perfectly happy with their ugly spreadsheet.

This is why I increasingly believe builders need to become comfortable with a slightly uncomfortable sentence: **just because I can build this does not mean I should.** The sentence does not make building less exciting. It makes the things that survive it much more interesting.
