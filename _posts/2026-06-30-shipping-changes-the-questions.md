Before a product ships, you can have incredibly sophisticated arguments about imaginary users. You can debate which feature people will love, whether a button is clear, what users will pay for and how often they will come back. Everybody can be right because none of it has happened yet. Shipping is useful because it begins killing theories.

[Koki](https://kokiafrique.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} changed for me the moment it stopped being something on my laptop and became something people could actually download. Before launch, the questions were mostly about construction: whether something worked, whether I could make a flow better and what still needed to be finished.

After launch, the questions became much more interesting. I wanted to know what people were actually using, where they were confused, what had looked important during development but barely mattered now, what happened on somebody else's phone, and which assumptions I had made because I knew how the app was supposed to work.

This is one of the reasons I think founders can stay in development for too long. Before launch, uncertainty is comfortable because you can continue solving it with more building. You can add another feature instead of finding out whether anyone cares about the current ones. You can redesign a screen instead of watching someone misunderstand it. You can postpone the possibility that the market may be less impressed than you are. Shipping removes some of that protection.

It does not magically give you perfect information. Early users can be unrepresentative, feedback can be contradictory, and some people ask for features they would never actually use. But at least you now have behaviour, and that changes the quality of the conversation.

I found the same thing outside software with Giiyo. There is a huge difference between planning what young people will respond to and standing in a room while the programme is actually happening. You notice which explanations land, where people lose attention, and what a school promised compared with what was actually available when you arrived. Reality keeps editing your plans.

I have started to think of shipping less as the end of building and more as the beginning of learning with consequences. Before shipping, changing your mind costs relatively little. After shipping, users, data and expectations exist. That makes decisions harder, but it also makes them better informed.

A product sitting in development can remain perfect because nobody has had the opportunity to prove otherwise. A product in the world has to become useful.
