One of the easiest ways to run a volunteer organisation badly is to keep reminding yourself that everybody is a volunteer. Someone misses a deadline and you say, “Well, they are volunteering.” Nobody knows who owns a task and you say, “We cannot expect too much.” A programme is approaching and important work has not been done, but you hesitate to follow up because people are giving their time for free. It sounds compassionate, but it can also become deeply unfair.

Giiyo Tech has depended on volunteers from the beginning, and there is no version of our growth where that is not true. People have taught, designed, communicated, travelled, coordinated programmes and given time that they could have used elsewhere. That deserves respect.

But respecting volunteers does not mean pretending expectations are unnecessary. In fact, I think the opposite is true. If somebody gives five hours of their week to an organisation, wasting those five hours because nobody planned properly is disrespectful. Inviting someone into a role without explaining what they own is disrespectful. Keeping reliable volunteers constantly cleaning up after unreliable ones is disrespectful.

The absence of a salary does not remove the need for management. Volunteer organisations still need role clarity. People need to know what they are responsible for, when something is due, who they should ask for help and what happens if they can no longer do the work. They also need feedback.

I think small organisations sometimes avoid feedback because it feels too formal. Everyone is friendly, everyone cares about the mission and nobody wants the organisation to start feeling corporate. Then resentment builds quietly. One person consistently delivers while another consistently disappears. The reliable person gets rewarded with more work because everybody knows they will do it, and the unreliable person gets fewer expectations because nobody wants an uncomfortable conversation. That is a terrible system, because it punishes reliability.

I have had to learn that leadership in a volunteer organisation cannot simply mean being grateful that people showed up. Gratitude matters, but it is not a management system. The organisation also owes volunteers something beyond a thank you. People should grow while they are with us. They should have meaningful responsibilities, opportunities to lead, evidence of the work they have done and a sense that their contribution matters. When somebody is consistently giving more, that should be recognised rather than treated as an unlimited resource.

This becomes even more important as Giiyo expands across cities. A small group in one room can survive on informal communication. An organisation running programmes in places like Limbe, Yaoundé and other parts of Cameroon cannot depend on one founder remembering every conversation and reminding everyone personally. The system has to be clear about who owns each programme, who speaks to the school, who confirms equipment, who follows up after the event, who communicates publicly and who makes a decision if the founder is not there. None of that is about becoming bureaucratic. It is about making it possible for good people to do good work without chaos getting in their way.

I also think volunteers should be allowed to leave. Sometimes people's lives change, a role stops fitting, their capacity disappears or they lose interest, and that is normal. What damages an organisation is not somebody saying, “I cannot do this anymore.” It is somebody remaining in a role on paper while everyone else quietly works around the fact that they are no longer doing it. Clear expectations actually make leaving easier because everybody knows what commitment was being made in the first place.

Giiyo will not always be as volunteer dependent as it is today. As the organisation grows, some roles need to become properly paid roles because the amount of responsibility involved cannot reasonably be sustained forever by goodwill. That transition matters to me, because I do not want “mission” to become an excuse for extracting work from people. But until we can pay everyone who deserves to be paid, the least we can do is manage their contribution properly.

Volunteers are not free labour. They are people choosing to invest part of their time in something you are building, and that makes the responsibility to use their time well even greater.
